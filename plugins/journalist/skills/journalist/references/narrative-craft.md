# Narrative Craft

The deep-dive companion to SKILL.md §3 "Every Piece Is a Journey." Load this when the piece needs to do narrative work, not just clean prose. The patterns here are operational — each section teaches a technique the writer can deploy.

For the workflow and the principle itself, see `../SKILL.md`.

---

## Show vs tell vs earn

Three levels of how the writer conveys meaning to the reader.

**Tell.** State the conclusion outright.

> She was furious.

**Show.** Present the evidence so vividly the reader cannot miss the inference.

> She slammed the binder shut.

**Earn.** Assemble the evidence over enough text that the reader reaches the conclusion themselves, without the writer ever stating it.

> She slammed the binder shut. The chair across the table cleared his throat — twice — and said nothing. The aide began collecting papers as if the meeting were already over. When she finally spoke, her voice was even, and that was how everyone in the room knew it.

The reader does not need the word *furious*. The reader has built it themselves. Earned meaning is held more firmly than told meaning, because the reader has done the work.

The skill is calibration. Some readers need more evidence; some need less. Genre matters — a hard-news lead earns nothing, it tells. A feature opener earns everything, telling almost nothing. The default for any piece meant to be *read* rather than scanned: earn whatever you can.

## Voice and point of view in nonfiction

The author's stance is always present, even in prose that reads as neutral. The choice is whether you use that stance deliberately or pretend it does not exist.

Four basic stances:

**The embedded narrator.** The writer is in the scene as observer. WSJ feature opener style.

> Outside the diner, Martha Renton stamped her boots in the snow. She had driven 90 miles for the meeting.

**The framed narrator.** The writer is outside the scene, narrating with the authority of having seen the whole. Most longform features. Most New Yorker pieces. The writer's perspective is in the framing — what's included, what's deferred, where the camera goes next.

**The omniscient.** The writer reports thoughts, intentions, internal states. Requires sourcing. Every claim about what someone thought should be traceable to a quote, a document, or a reconstructed conversation the writer can defend.

**The impersonal.** Wire copy. The writer disappears entirely. Information is the protagonist. Useful when the facts are the story and any narrative voice would distract.

"I" earns its place when the writer is genuinely part of the story — as participant, witness, or person whose interpretation matters. "I" does not earn its place when the writer slips into autobiography because the prose has slowed down and they need something to say. Cut the autobiographical detours.

## The Chekhov principle in nonfiction

The Russian playwright's rule: if a gun is on the wall in act 1, it must go off by act 3.

In nonfiction, the rule runs both directions:

- **Forward**: anything noticed early in the piece — an image, a contradiction, a character, a statistic — should pay off later. If it does not, cut it. The reader has been carrying it, expecting return.
- **Backward**: anything that pays off late should be planted earlier. The reveal works *because* the reader has been carrying the planted detail without knowing why.

Apply this when revising. Mark every concrete detail and ask: does it return? If not, decide whether to cut it or to give it a return appearance. The piece tightens either way.

## The reveal

The strongest pieces have at least one moment where the apparent meaning shifts. Surface observation turns out to be the symptom of something deeper. The conventional explanation turns out to be wrong. The character the reader trusted turns out to be the antagonist.

Plan for the reveal before drafting. The piece is built around it. The lede gestures at it without giving it away. The middle accumulates evidence that, in retrospect, was always pointing at the reveal. The reveal lands, and the reader does the work of seeing what it changes.

A reveal cannot be told. It must be earned by what precedes it. The sentence that contains the reveal is often understated — the structural work has already been done; the sentence just lets the reader see it.

For feature-length pieces with no reveal, the question is whether the piece needs to exist in this form. A piece without a reveal might be better as a list, a chart, or a memo. The reveal is what justifies the prose form.

## Building tension

Three techniques:

**Withhold strategically.** The reader does not need every fact at once. A name held for two paragraphs is more powerful than a name given in the first sentence. A consequence revealed slowly weighs more than one announced in summary. The writer's restraint becomes the reader's investment.

**Foreshadow consequences.** Before the bad thing happens, plant the conditions that make it inevitable. The reader feels the dread before the explosion. By the time the explosion arrives, it has been earned.

**Switch scales.** Move from the granular to the panoramic and back. The single person at the diner. The pattern of millions of people. The single person again, now seen as one of the millions. Sustained zoom in either direction fatigues. The shift is what holds attention.

## Connective tissue patterns

Named patterns for moving between paragraphs:

**Causal chain.** "X happened. As a result, Y. Which meant Z." Strongest connection. Use when the logic genuinely is causal.

**Compare and contrast.** "But." "By contrast." "On the other hand." Earned when there is genuine tension or surprise in the contrast. Cheapened when used as filler.

**Scale shift.** "Across the country, the pattern was the same." "For one family, that meant something specific." Pivots between general and particular. Best when both halves are needed.

**Time jump.** "Three years later." "By the time the report was published." Works when the transition itself carries meaning. A bare time-marker is weak; a time-marker with stakes is strong.

When in doubt, the test: can the reader say "and so" between the paragraphs? If yes, the connection is real. If the answer is closer to "and also," the connection is wiki accretion and the paragraphs need restructuring.

## Marrying research and prose

Three failure modes:

**The research dump.** Three paragraphs of cited statistics with no through-line. Looks substantive; reads as a press release. The reader skims and forgets.

**The anecdote without weight.** A single specific scene that does not connect to anything larger. Pleasant to read, leaves no impression.

**The argument without evidence.** Opinion stated with confidence and no support. Reads as bluster.

The successful pattern: a scene grounds the reader in something specific. A fact reframes what the scene means. The implication earns its place because both have been shown.

> Outside the diner, Martha Renton stamped her boots in the snow. She had driven 90 miles for the meeting. *(Scene.)*
>
> In rural counties across the state, nearly one in three primary-care visits now requires a drive of more than 60 miles. *(Fact.)*
>
> The closures are not a side effect of the rural health crisis. They are the crisis. *(Implication.)*

Each element earns the next. The scene buys the fact; the fact reframes the scene; the implication earns its place because the reader has seen both halves.

## The wiki test

Read your draft. Pick any three adjacent paragraphs. Can you shuffle them without breaking the piece? If yes, you have written a wiki entry, not a piece.

A wiki entry collects facts about a subject. The order does not matter; the reader is using it as a lookup. A piece moves a reader from one understanding to another. The order is the meaning.

If you fail the wiki test, the fix is structural. Identify the spine — the argument or causal chain the piece is actually about — and rebuild the paragraphs to follow it. Some paragraphs will not survive the restructuring. Cut them.

The related test: read the piece and ask whether each paragraph could be deleted without anyone noticing. If yes, cut it. A paragraph that does not advance the journey is not protecting the journey by sitting there; it is dragging the journey down.

## Patterns from masters

Not a syllabus; signposts for readers who want to study how this is done.

**John McPhee** wrote structure tables before he wrote drafts — sketches mapping which sections went where, how they connected, what the through-line was. Every McPhee piece has a structural intelligence that did not happen by accident. Read *Draft No. 4* for his account of the method.

**Joan Didion** wrote first paragraphs that contained the entire piece in microcosm. The reader who finished her opening paragraph already knew what the piece would be about, what mood would govern it, and what the writer's relationship to the material would be. Study her ledes.

**Michael Lewis** built large arguments around a single character — usually an outsider whose perspective revealed the system. *The Big Short.* *Moneyball.* *Flash Boys.* The character is the lens; the system is the subject; the reader is brought to the system by following the character.

**Patrick Radden Keefe**, in *Empire of Pain* and *Say Nothing*, deployed withholding at scale — characters introduced ambiguously, revelations timed across hundreds of pages, the meaning of entire books shifting in the final chapters. Study how the timing of information shapes meaning.

The common thread: each of these writers thinks at the structural level before they think at the sentence level. Strong sentences in a poorly structured piece will not save it. A well-structured piece tolerates rough sentences; rough structure cannot be saved by even the strongest sentences.

Structure first. Sentences second. Always.
